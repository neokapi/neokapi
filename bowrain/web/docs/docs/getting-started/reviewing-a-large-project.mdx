# Reviewing a large project

A project connected to a real repository holds thousands of blocks in the
source language, and as many again in each target language. You will not read
them one by one, and you don't need to. Most of that text is generated from a
few decisions: where each part of the project sits, which terms it uses, and
how its voice sounds. Decide those, and the text follows.

The **review plan** on each project puts those decisions in order. Each step
reaches more content than the step below it, so you start at the top.

## The five steps

| Step | What you decide | How much one decision reaches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Coordinates | the products, channels and languages, and the voice profile each part of the project uses | every block in that part |
| 2. Key language | the project's terms and voice rules, in the source language first, then each term's translation | every block that uses the term or meets the rule |
| 3. Exceptions | content that fails a check, grouped by the rule it breaks | the blocks that rule names |
| 4. Interfaces in context | whole screens, components and pages, read where they ship | the strings on the page |
| 5. Settled by the checks | nothing: content that clears every check is established under your policy | |

### 1. Coordinates

The plan lists each part of the project with its product and channel, the
voice profile bound there, and the languages the project translates into.
These come from the project's recipe or from the context scan Bowrain runs
when you connect a repository. Read them once and confirm them. A product
filed under the wrong voice profile, or a language the project never ships,
puts every block below it at the wrong point.

On the phone, the step opens a map of the project: products as rows, channels
as columns. Each cell shows how many blocks sit there, the voice profile bound
there, and a bar for each target language showing how much of it is
established. The grid scrolls sideways when a product has many channels. Tap a
cell to see its collections.

When you confirm, Bowrain records the map as it stands: the parts of the
project, their voice profiles and the target languages. If the recipe changes
later, the plan lists each change against that record, under the name of the
person who confirmed: a new part, a part removed or moved, a different voice
profile, a language added or removed. The map outlines the changed cells, and
the plan marks the coordinates as the next step again.

Bowrain keeps one record per project. Anyone who can edit the project's
settings can confirm, and one confirmation settles the step for the whole
team. A change someone has confirmed leaves everyone's plan.

### 2. Key language

The context deck holds the terms and voice rules that wait for your review.
Decide each term in the source language first, then its term in each language
you review, while you know what the term means.

To find the terms a project uses, choose **Scan the project for terms** on this
step. Bowrain reads a sample of every collection in the source language and
proposes the terms it uses most, ranked by how many blocks use each one. They
arrive first in the context deck: keep a term, or mark it as not a term so it
is not proposed again.

A term you keep governs every block that uses it: the checks hold the source
to it, translation uses it, and a translation that misses it fails. A term
that has no translation in a language yet is counted on the plan, because
until you decide one, every translation of that term is a guess.

When you keep a term, Bowrain proposes its term in each target language and
deals the proposal to you as the next card, in the languages you review. The
card shows the source term, the proposed term, the language and how many
blocks use the term. Keep it, edit the wording and keep yours, or choose
**Not this term**. A proposal you turn down is not proposed again. A proposal
counts for nothing until you keep it, so translation never uses a term nobody
has reviewed.

#### Sessions on the phone

On the phone, the key language comes in sessions of ten cards. The project
has one queue for the whole team. A session takes the ten cards that reach the
most blocks and holds them for you while it runs, so a colleague working at
the same time gets different cards. A card anyone decides leaves every queue.

A session opens with what changed since the team's last session: terms a scan
found, terms now used in more blocks, and how many cards others decided. The
feed shows their decisions, and nothing asks you to decide them again. Each
card shows how many blocks use the term, as a bar against the widest term in
the session, the channels it appears in, and the term proposed for each
language you review.

When you finish, the summary counts what you kept, changed and turned down,
and how many blocks use the terms you kept. Choose **Ten more** for the next
session or **Done for now** to stop. Leaving the screen ends the session and
returns its cards to the queue. A session nobody touches for half an hour ends
on its own.

A second person reviews a decision only when your workspace turns on
separation of duties. Then Bowrain assigns that review as its own step.

### 3. Exceptions

The plan counts the findings in the source and the translations that fail a
check. Most findings are one rule broken many times, so the plan lists the
rules behind the most findings: for the source, as the last project-wide check
counted them, and for each language you review. Each rule shows its count and
what it names, such as a term the content uses or a voice pattern it matches.

Decide each rule before its blocks. If the rule is wrong, change it, and every
finding it raised clears. If the content is wrong, fix the source, and the
translations are drafted again from it. On the phone, tap a language's rule to
open that language's deck with only the translations that break it. What
remains after the rules are decided, you decide block by block.

### 4. Interfaces in context

Read complete interfaces where they ship: a component in its running story, a
document section with its heading. Read the source first, then each
translation of the same page. On the phone, a language's deck opens on pages
for this reason.

How many pages you read depends on your involvement (below). Under
**Context**, the plan deals a sample: one page in ten from each collection,
and every page with a translation that fails a check. That is enough to
confirm the decisions above read right.

### 5. Settled by the checks

Everything else is settled by the checks you have already decided. A
translation that passes the checks, uses the terms and meets the voice bar is
established as your decision: it is recorded in the decision ledger under your
name with the reason "review policy", and anyone who rejects it later reopens
it. The plan's last step shows how many translations qualify and establishes
them when you ask.

A project's first pass settles on the checks alone, so you can establish
what passes before the key language is finished. After that, the step waits
until the key language is decided: every term reviewed, with a term in each
target language. Until then the plan shows how many decisions are still
open.

## The languages you review

Each person chooses the languages they review in each project. Your Review
tab and the plan count only those languages. Until you choose, you follow
every language your membership lets you review.

On the phone, the Review tab shows each project you follow as five rings, one
per step. A ring fills with the team's progress through its step and shows
what still waits there. Tap a ring to open that step. The chip at the top of
the tab names the languages you follow and opens their settings, which are
also under **Languages you review** on the Account tab.

The first time you open a project's plan, Bowrain asks which languages you
review. If you manage the project, the same screen also shows its
coordinates, its target languages and its involvement. Turning a target
language on or off files a recipe change: the next sync writes it into the
project's recipe, and the feed records it.

Following a language never copies work. Everyone who follows a language
shares its one queue, and a translation one person decides leaves the queue
for everyone.

## Choosing your involvement

Set how much your reviewers can be involved, and the plan follows:

| Involvement | You read | The checks settle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Context | the coordinates, the key language, the exceptions and a sample of pages | every translation that clears every check |
| Interfaces | as Context, and every interface page | the translations the pages do not cover |
| Every translation | everything | nothing |

**Context** is the default, and it is the setting a single reviewer can keep
up with on a large project. Choose **Interfaces** when a team can read every
screen before a release, and **Every translation** for content where each
sentence carries legal or safety weight. Changing it needs the permission to
edit the project's settings.

## Getting the source right first

Every translation is made from the source, so the source comes first at every
step: its terms before their translations, its findings before the
translations' findings, its reading of a page before each translation of that
page. Translation keeps running while you review the source; a source fix
drafts the affected translations again on its own.
